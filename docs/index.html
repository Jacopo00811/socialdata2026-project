The Paradox of Happiness

Why do the happiest countries in Europe consume the most antidepressants? A data-driven investigation across 21 nations.

Social Data Analysis & Visualization · DTU 2026

↓

Are the Happiness Rankings a Lie?

Year after year, Nordic nations top the World Happiness Report [5]. School is free. Healthcare is universal. Parental leave is generous. Streets are safe, corruption is rare, and trust in government and society runs deep. By almost every measure, these are the best places on Earth to be alive. And yet, these same countries also lead the OECD in antidepressant consumption [6], prescribing up to 140 defined daily doses per 1,000 people every single day.[4]

This is what is called "The Paradox of Happiness": the places where people say they are happiest are also where they take the most medication for depression. Are the happiness rankings a lie? Are these countries hiding collective misery behind a pharmaceutical veil? Or is something more subtle at work?

To investigate, we assembled data on 21 European countries from five sources: the World Happiness Report (2015-2020) [5], World Bank GINI coefficients (inequality) [3], World Bank unemployment rates [2], OECD antidepressant prescription statistics [6], and the European Social Survey Round 9 (2018) [4] for institutional trust and social connectedness. We averaged across years to filter out the annual noise and expose the deeper structural patterns that define each nation

The chart below makes the paradox unmistakable. On the left, countries ranked by happiness. On the right, the same countries ranked by antidepressant usage. Notice how the blue bars, representing the majority of Scandinavian nations, cluster near the top half of both lists.

Figure 1. Countries sorted independently by Happiness (left) and Antidepressant usage in DDD/1,000/day (right). Blue = Scandinavian countries. Iceland, the happiest and most medicated, is very high in both charts.
DDD (Defined Daily Dose) is the W.H.O. [7] standard unit for drug consumption. A value of 50 DDD/1,000/day means 50 out of every 1,000 people take one full maintenance dose of an antidepressant on any given day.

The left side holds few surprises. Nordic nations sit where we expect them, happiness scores fading as you move south and east. But the right side is stranger. Latvia, one of Europe's least happy countries, is also its least medicated. That fits the story. Portugal does not: ranking near the bottom in happiness, it still prescribes antidepressants at nearly Icelandic rates. The paradox, it turns out, is not perfectly clean and that makes it more interesting, not less.

Inequality (GINI) [3] and unemployment [2] show their own characteristic spreads, with Southern European outliers pulling the tails.

What Correlates with Happiness?

Before we can solve the paradox, we need to rule out the obvious. Wealth, inequality and unemployment are the usual suspects whenever happiness enters the conversation. So we put them to the test.

The matrix below maps every pairwise relationship among our four variables. Happiness, antidepressant use, inequality, and unemployment. Each cell is a question: do these two things move together across Europe? The red line tells you the direction. The R² tells you how strongly.

Figure 3. Pairwise scatter matrix with OLS regression lines and R² values. The diagonal shows kernel density estimates. The positive slope in the Happiness-Antidepressants cell (upper-right area) is the paradox visualized as a trend line.

Equality and employment leave their mark on happiness that much holds. But the moment you ask what drives antidepressant use, the economic story goes quiet. Medication has virtually no relationship with how unequal a country is, and none at all with how many people are out of work. The countries that medicate most are not simply the ones where life is easiest.

And yet the paradox refuses to budge. Inequality explains happiness to a degree, but it does not explain medication. Unemployment barely registers. The two variables that should dissolve the paradox turn out to have almost nothing to say about it. The answer has to lie somewhere else entirely.

Three Europes

Correlations only tell you so much. To see the fuller picture, we let the data sort itself by running a clustering algorithm across all 21 countries and all four metrics simultaneously. No assumptions about geography, no preconceived groupings. We simply asked the data: which countries are most alike?

Three distinct groups emerged. And when you see which countries land together, the geography writes itself almost perfectly onto the map of Europe.

The clusters tell a compelling story:

Cluster 0 — "The Happy North" (11 countries): Austria, Belgium, Czechia, Denmark, Finland, Germany, Iceland, Netherlands, Norway, Sweden, UK. High happiness (7.25), low GINI (28.6), low unemployment (5.2%), high antidepressants (78 DDD).

Cluster 1 — "The Struggling South" (6 countries): France, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Portugal, Spain. They present higher inequality and unemployment, moderate medication. Avg happiness (6.10), mid GINI (34.7), high unemployment (10.3%), mid antidepressants (55 DDD).

Cluster 2 — "The Eastern Middle" (4 countries): Estonia, Hungary, Slovakia, Slovenia. Represent low inequality and unemployment like the north, but low happiness and low medication. Avg happiness (5.93), low GINI (27.7), low unemployment (6.2%), low antidepressants (41 DDD).
Figure 5. K-Means clusters (K=3, selected via silhouette analysis) projected into 3D PCA space. Drag to rotate. The three principal components capture 89% of total variance. The closeness of some Eastern Middle (cluster 2) countries to cluster 0 confirms their similar structural profile.

Not a Fluke

A single averaged value might be a fluke. But what if we watch the paradox unfold year by year? The animated scatter below tracks each country from 2015 to 2020 through Antidepressant Use vs. Happiness score, with bubble size encoding unemployment rate.

Figure 6a. Animated scatter plot (2015-2020). Each bubble is a country. X = Antidepressant DDD, Y = Happiness, Size = Unemployment rate, Colour = Cluster. Notice how Portugal drifts right (rising antidepressant use) without gaining too much happiness for its final DDD value.

You can notice three trends: a slight increase in happiness across all countries, an overall shift to the right (higher antidepressant use), and a slight decrease in unemployment (shrinking bubbles). The paradox is growing, structural, and not momentary. Pay particular attention to Portugal and Spain: over the years they become outliers to the paradox, ending up in the top consumer of antidepressants while maintaining low happiness scores. All other countries tend to stay in their area, maintaining roughly the same distances to neighbouring points.

And the rankings tell the same story even more starkly. While unemployment shifted, economies wobbled, and the world changed around them, the order at the top of the happiness table barely moved. Finland, Denmark, Iceland trade places with each other year after year, but they never leave. The blue lines hold.

Figure 6b. Happiness rankings over 2015-2020 (1 = happiest). The top spots are dominated by cluster 0 countries, while the bottom half features Southern and Eastern European countries.

But stability looks different depending on where you stand. Zoom out to the map of Europe and the modest changes of the last five years reveal a quiet shift the countries that gained the most ground were not the ones already at the top.

Figure 6c. Net happiness score change (2015-2020). Green markers = improvement, Red = decline. Click any marker for full details. The overall happiness across Europe increased, with only minimal drops in Belgium and Norway.

Hungary, Slovenia, and the Baltic states nudged upward, closing the gap slightly from below. Portugal, despite its stubbornly high antidepressant use, also managed a small gain. The Nordic nations barely moved because they had nowhere much to go. The ceiling, it seems, is high but it is not unreachable.

The Explanation: It's About Trust

We've established the paradox and shown it's partially true, persistent throughout the years, and has some geographic patterns. But we haven't explained why there are still some outliers and if we can find a better predictor. For that, we turn to the European Social Survey (Round 9, 2018) [4] one of the highest-quality cross-national surveys in the world, with ~50,000 respondents answering detailed questions about their trust in institutions, social connections, and civic engagement.

From six ESS [4] variables, we constructed two composite indices:

Institutional Trust = z-score average of: Trust in People (0-10), Trust in Politicians (0-10), and Satisfaction with Democracy (0-10).

Social Connection = z-score average of: Frequency of Discussing Politics, Frequency of Social Meetings, and Participation in Social Activities.

The result is the single most striking finding in our analysis:

Institutional Trust explains 75% of the cross-country variation in happiness (R² = 0.749, p < 0.0001).

This makes every other predictor we examined very weak. Not GINI (R² = 0.20), not unemployment (R² = 0.12), not antidepressants (R² = 0.20). Trust is the master variable. Countries where people trust their government, their neighbors, and their democratic institutions are overwhelmingly happier.

And here is, in our point of view, the key to resolving the paradox: these same high-trust societies have destigmatized mental healthcare. When you trust your doctor, your health system, and your society not to judge you, you're far more likely to seek help for depression or mental health issues, and that help often comes in the form of antidepressants. The high prescription rates in the cluster 0 don't signify artificial happiness; they signal a society that takes mental health as seriously as physical health. Sweden, Denmark, and Finland rank as the three most responsive European countries in mental healthcare [8], the same countries that top our happiness and antidepressant charts.

Portugal tells a different story. The outlier with high antidepressant use but low happiness also shows much lower trust levels, suggesting that medication there may be an automatic response to untreated mental health issues rather than a sign of awareness and care. When the system is not trusted, pills can become a substitute for support rather than part of it.

Figure 7. Institutional Trust (z-score composite from ESS Round 9) vs. Happiness Score. R² = 0.749. Dashed line = OLS fit. Bubble size = antidepressant DDD. The largest bubbles (highest medication) cluster in the upper-right: high trust, high happiness. Finland, Iceland, Sweden, and Denmark exemplify the pattern perfectly. However, Norway and the Netherlands show that the link between trust and antidepressant consumption is not absolute. They have high trust and happiness but lower antidepressant use compared to France, Spain, and Portugal and the UK.

The Cluster Profiles Confirm It

The radar chart below shows how each cluster scores across all six raw ESS variables (min-max normalized to 0-1). Cluster 0: The "happy, medicated" Nordic archetype, dominates on every single dimension of trust and social engagement. They discuss politics more, meet friends more often, participate in more social activities, trust people more, trust politicians more, and are more satisfied with their democracy.

Clusters 1 and 2 each have a distinct profile. Cluster 1 (Continental/Southern) has a notable spike in social meetings and activities, while Cluster 2 (Eastern) is more balanced overall but shows little to no satisfaction with democracy. The gap isn't in wealth or weather; it's in social fabric.

Figure 8. Social fabric profiles by K-Means cluster (K=3). Each axis represents one ESS variable (min-max normalized). Cluster 0 (Nordic/Northwestern) has the largest, most rounded polygon presenting high scores on every dimension of social capital. Cluster 1 (Continental/Southern) spikes on social meetings and activities. Cluster 2 (Eastern) is the smallest polygon overall, with notably low democratic satisfaction.
The resolution: The “Paradox of Happiness” is not always true, but it is often satisfied. Antidepressant usage is not the only measure to indicate a country's happiness, look at Portugal, Spain, Norway, and the UK, they are clear outliers. The safest single predictor of a country's happiness is Institutional Trust, which has the highest correlation of all the metrics compared. High antidepressant use in happy countries reflects accessible, destigmatized mental healthcare within high-trust societies, not hidden despair. Antidepressant usage should be combined with other metrics for higher predictive power.
