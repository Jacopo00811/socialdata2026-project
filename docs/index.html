The Drug That Is Taking Over Them All

How opioids, particularly fentanyl, are reshaping San Francisco's drug landscape. Why more policing may not be the answer?

A data story based on San Francisco Police Department incident reports from 2003 to 2025

↓

Setting the Scene

San Francisco has long wrestled with drug-related crime, but the trends you see in media headlines can be deceptive. To understand what's actually changed, we looked at every drug offense logged by the San Francisco Police Department from 2003 to early 2025. That's around 150,000 incidents spanning more than two decades.

Data comes from two publicly available SFPD datasets: the historical reports (2003-2018) and the modern one (2018-present), merged and cleaned for consistency. Each record tells us what drug was involved, and when and where the incident happened[1].

An important caveat: Police incident data reflects the police activity, not actual drug use and distribution. Therefore, as Richardson et al.[2] remind us, an increase in "cocaine offenses" might not mean more cocaine, it might just be that the police ran a cocaine-focused operation in those times.
Crime data is always shaped by the choices of the institution collecting it.

With that in mind, the story, spanning 22 years, focuses on five major drug categories dominated SFPD reports: cocaine, marijuana, methamphetamine, heroin, and a "opiates". That last category is the key to this story and what is effecting the city the most. In particular this category includes fentanyl, a powerful synthetic opioid that has been zombifying its users and causing an increase in overdose deaths in the city. The situation reached such a critical state that the mayor had to declare a state of emergency for the Tenderloin district[3].

The Great Reshuffle

The chart below shows how the composition of drug offenses has transformed over time. Each band represents one drug category's share of all SFPD drug incidents that year. Also marked on the graph are key policy milestones: the 2016 marijuana legalization, the time when fentanyl began to surge, and the 2021 Tenderloin emergency declaration[3].
Note how some crime reports might classify fentanyl as part of the "Narcotics (general)" category as it is indeed a narcotic drug used for its analgesic properties.

Figure 1. Percentage share of five major drug categories in all SFPD drug-offense incidents, 2003-2025. Cocaine (amber) and marijuana (blue) dominated until the late 2010s. After marijuana legalization in 2016, cannabis offenses nearly vanished. Meanwhile, synthetic drugs like Opiates (pink) and Methamphetamine (green) barely were reported before 2010, and then became the fastest-growing categories until 2021-2022.

In 2006, cocaine alone accounted for nearly 40% of all drug offenses. By 2023, the "Opiates" category, without counting the general Narcotics category, has surged from under 1% to over 16%.

The crossover to new drugs is unprecedented: as heroin, marijuana and cocaine incidents collapsed, opiates and narcotics exploded accounting for a total of 63.6% of total reported drug offenses in 2025. This mirrors what public health researchers have documented: illicitly manufactured fentanyl and other synthetic narcotics entered San Francisco's drug supply around 2019[4], rapidly replacing heroin and other drugs on the streets.

According to the San Francisco Office of the Chief Medical Examiner, of the 1,833 opioid-involved overdose deaths between 2020 and 2023, 1,686 (92%) tested positive for fentanyl or a fentanyl analog[6].

The district-level view reveals something the citywide chart obscures: the opioid crisis is not a San Francisco-wide phenomenon, it is a Tenderloin phenomenon. Of all opiate and narcotics related incidents since 2020, the majority occurred in the Tenderloin district alone, followed by Mission, and Southern. The remaining seven districts share a combined minimal fraction of incident reports.

Does More Enforcement Help?

The surge in opiate incidents after 2019 coincides with a major escalation in drug enforcement. After December 2021, when the Mayor declared a state of emergency in the Tenderloin, in early 2022 the District Attorney implemented new policies to "bundle misdemeanor drug possession charges"[8]. In 2023, the California Governor authorized a multiagency operation specifically targeting fentanyl trafficking[9].

The paradox: A 2025 study published in JAMA Network Open found that law enforcement drug seizures in San Francisco were associated with a 74% increase in the relative risk of a fatal opioid overdose within 100 meters the day after the seizure. The elevated risk persisted for up to 7 days[4].

How can seizing drugs increase overdose deaths? The researchers propose a mechanism: when police arrest a dealer and seize their supply, the people who depended on that dealer — many of whom are physically dependent on opioids — must urgently find a new source. The replacement drugs may be more potent, differently composed, or simply unfamiliar, dramatically increasing overdose risk[6].

As the FilterMag summary of this research puts it: "To our knowledge, no evidence-based approach exists that shows supply-side interventions, such as policing and seizing drugs, reduces drug use or drug-related health problems" [5].

The researchers recommend evidence-based alternatives: easier access to medications for opioid use disorder, safe supply programs, drug decriminalization, and supervised consumption sites — all of which have been shown to reduce overdose deaths without increasing crime [6]. One often-cited example is Portugal, which in 2001 decriminalized the possession of small amounts of drugs and shifted its approach toward treatment and harm reduction rather than punishment. Individuals are referred to “dissuasion commissions” composed of health and social work professionals instead of entering the criminal justice system.

This reform was paired with increased investment in addiction treatment and public health services. In the years following the policy change, Portugal experienced significant reductions in overdose deaths and HIV transmission, while overall drug use did not increase [10]. The case suggests that reducing harm does not necessarily depend on eliminating drug use, but on making drug use less dangerous.

Whether San Francisco adopts such approaches remains uncertain, but the data suggests that continuing to rely on enforcement alone may not be enough.

What the data can — and can't — tell us

It's important to be transparent about limitations. The explosion of "opiates" in SFPD data could partly reflect more enforcement rather than more drug use. The Tenderloin emergency declaration almost certainly increased the volume of police contacts in that neighborhood. As discussed by Richardson et al.[2], arrest data is shaped by policing priorities. A neighborhood where police are sent more often will generate more incident reports, regardless of whether actual drug use has increased.

However, dead bodies cannot be explained by policing strategy changes. The fact that 92% of opioid overdose deaths in recent years involved fentanyl [7] confirms what the police data suggests: Fentanyl is transforming San Francisco's drug landscape.

Taken together, the data and the public health evidence point to the same conclusion: San Francisco's drug crisis has not simply intensified — it has fundamentally changed. Cocaine, heroin, and marijuana, which dominated the streets for decades, have been displaced in just a few years by fentanyl, a synthetic opioid far more potent and far more deadly than anything that came before it.

Understanding this transformation is essential. Because if the nature of the crisis has changed, the response to it must change as well.
